# Websites and content

## Match the visitor's purpose

For a marketing page, help the visitor understand the offer, evaluate credible evidence, and take a clear action. For a portfolio or gallery, give the actual work visual priority. For an article or documentation page, prioritize reading rhythm, orientation, and navigation. A single website may contain all of these surfaces.

Open with the strongest subject-specific content: a product in use, a concrete headline, a significant artifact, a useful demonstration, or imagery whose subject matters. Do not build a badge, headline, two buttons, and three cards before deciding what the page needs to say.

Compose sections from their content relationships. Vary rhythm where the narrative warrants it; do not alternate section colors or introduce asymmetry mechanically. Keep important content available in the semantic reading order at narrow widths.

## Art direction without arbitrary bans

Give the surface a clear visual thesis grounded in the audience and subject. Look for one memorable compositional or material choice, then make type, spacing, images, and interaction support it. Match reference images through their hierarchy, proportions, lettering character, and material treatment, not just a sampled palette.

Common defaults worth questioning include unrelated glow backgrounds, generic cream-and-serif luxury styling, meaningless metric heroes, uniform feature-card grids, repeated eyebrow labels, decorative section numbers, and identical fade-up reveals. These are diagnostic prompts. An explicit brief or real content can justify them; replacing one fashion with an obligatory industrial or newspaper style is another default.

Do not redesign an established site during a polish task. For an authorized redesign, preserve functional routes, meaningful content, product claims, and required brand assets while deliberately replacing the visual decisions in scope.

## Assets and truthful content

Inventory real photography, illustrations, product captures, video, and vector assets. Use raster imagery for texture, place, photographic subjects, or complex illustration; use HTML, SVG, or canvas for exact data and editable relationships. Do not imitate a real product screenshot with decorative rectangles or put navigation and labels inside a generated image.

When an original image is needed, define subject, composition, crop, placement, negative space, output size, and relevant visual constraints. Use the available generation tool according to its instructions, inspect the result, and optimize it for delivery. If production media is unavailable, identify the specific missing asset and provide an honest fallback.

Keep claims, testimonials, client logos, pricing, and metrics grounded in supplied or verified facts. Mark demo content where it could be mistaken for real results. Case studies should explain context, constraints, decisions, artifacts, evidence, and limitations without inventing outcomes.

## Delivery details

Test heading wraps, navigation, image crops, long content, and sticky elements across representative widths. Reserve media space, prioritize the real hero asset, and defer lower-page media when appropriate. Keep large decorative assets from blocking useful content.

For a new public page, check its title, description, canonical destination, social preview, favicon, indexability, and actual link destinations where applicable. Preserve deployment infrastructure and publish only within existing authorization. A local preview does not establish production behavior.
